Length as priority, not constraint: search geometry of the Andrews-Curtis Challenge pool

This is a partial result. It makes no claim about the Andrews-Curtis conjecture in either direction. It reports measurements over the 10,115 Discovery pool presentations that fix how an AC search should be structured, a structural obstruction limiting what any search of this family can score, and one verified non-existence statement at a bounded length.

Write P for a balanced presentation on x and y, L(P) for its total relator length, and d(P) for the moves applied. Every path below was replayed by the official reference verifier, and the move numbering checked equal to the official core.apply_move on 38,682 pairs.


1. A HARD BOUND ON TOTAL LENGTH IS THE DOMINANT DESIGN ERROR

Bidirectional search here is naturally written with a cap: reject any state whose L(P) exceeds the start length plus a slack s. An AC path characteristically lengthens the relators before they collapse, so the cap excludes exactly the states a short path must cross.

Replacing the cap with an ordering, the priority f(P) = L(P) + w*d(P) with no admission test, changes the outcome. On ten presentations of known optimum, at a fixed node budget, the path length returned was:

   challenge    optimum    w=1    w=4    w=12
   ac-00236        15       16     15     15
   ac-00343        15       17     15     15
   ac-00488        14       15     14     14
   ac-00742        10       11     10     10
   ac-00807        14       17     14     14
   ac-00894        14       17     15     14
   ac-01143        16       17     17     16
   ac-01411        11       16     11     11
   ac-01586        13       15     13     13
   ac-01635         8       10      8      8
   exact                   0/10   8/10   10/10

At w=12 all ten are solved at exactly the known optimum, in 0.4 to 2.6 seconds using 2,028 to 2,456,933 states. The capped search needed millions of states and 5 to 25 seconds per presentation to do worse.

The depth charge buys exactness and is paid for in states: w=1 finds a path in 177 to 858 states but never the optimum, w=4 takes 1,118 to 80,046 and reaches eight of ten. With no charge, length alone orders the queue and paths are far from minimal: 32 against an optimum of 15, and 129 against 14.

Widening the cap is no substitute for removing it: on a presentation with record 91, slack 2 returned 95 and slack 4 nothing. A wider box grows faster than any budget covers.


2. THE TWO TERMS OF THE PRIORITY TRADE OFF SHARPLY

No single w serves the pool, because f is at least w*d, which bounds reachable depth. At w=12 every record of 16 or less is solved exactly, and 0 of 35 at record 121 or more. At w=0 those are reached but the search wanders: 1,912 moves against record 121, and 3,742 against 301. Intermediate values do neither. A search of this form is competent on a band, not a pool.

Nor does shortening rescue a poor path. Detour replacement converges immediately, 1,912 to 415 to 396 to 396, and gains only about 2 per cent on already short paths.


3. THE BACKWARD HALF NEVER READS THE PRESENTATION

In a bidirectional search the half expanding from the trivial pair is constrained only by the length bound, so every presentation of a given size rebuilds an identical state set. This is visible in node counts: 7,226,201 recurred across four distinct presentations in one run, and again in another. Building that set once per bound reduces each presentation to its forward search alone.

It also bounds precomputation. One set built at length 40 or less, with 22,000,000 states in 19 seconds, contained 2 of the 10,115 pool presentations. Under a tight bound branching is low and the set reaches deep; under a loose bound 22,000,000 states buy only 8 to 10 moves of depth, while the pool lies 20 to 130 moves from the trivial pair. No single bound is both wide enough to contain the pool and tight enough to reach it.


4. HARDNESS STRATIFICATION

Of the 156 challenges held, grouped by the number of teams k sharing the record: k=10, 1 challenge, median 8; k=8, 48 challenges, median 15; k=3, 19, median 26; k=2, 20, median 26 with maximum 48. A record is short and widely shared precisely because its optimum is easy to reach, and long and singly held precisely because it is not.


5. COMPETENCE AND POINT VALUE ARE ANTI-CORRELATED BY CONSTRUCTION

A tie pays 2^(1-k), so matching a record held by one team pays 0.5 and one shared six ways pays 0.008. Since the short records are the widely shared ones, the records a search can reach are exactly those worth almost nothing.

Measured with the solver of section 1 at w=12 and 12,000,000 states per side:

   record 18 or less, k = 6 to 13, a match pays about 0.008: reached reliably
   record 19 with k = 6, a match pays 0.008: reached
   record 19 to 30 with k of 1 or 2, a match pays 0.25 to 0.50: 0 of 16

In records 19 to 30 alone there are 73 unheld challenges at k = 1 and 45 at k = 2, about 48 points, all beyond the competence boundary. Consistently: nothing on 21 attempts at records 27 to 40, nothing on 35 at 121 to 300, nothing on 17 above 301.

This is stronger than "the search is too weak": competence and value are anti-correlated by construction, so no target selection within a fixed boundary escapes it, only moving the boundary does. Improving a heavily shared record was also futile in every case checked: for a record-8 presentation shared ten ways the search returns 8 at every slack from 1 to 8, witnessing optimality, and zero of 61 attempts improved anything.


5b. ASKING THE DECISION QUESTION INSTEAD OF THE OPTIMISATION QUESTION

Every search above optimises, spending most of its budget on lengths that could not score. Only one question pays, and it is binary: does a path of at most the record length exist? Starting the iterative-deepening bound at the record asks exactly that, with the upper bound free from the weighted A* above. Measured on unheld challenges with k of 1 or 2 at records 21 to 30, memory linear in depth, budget 150,000,000 states:

   ac-07351, record 24: space exhausted at 76,000,000 states
   ac-06403, record 22: budget consumed, undecided
   ac-02353, record 24: budget consumed, undecided

The first line is a genuine, if small, negative result. Within the word cap of 42 there is no Andrews-Curtis path of length at most 24 for that presentation, and the search terminated by exhaustion rather than by running out of budget. At scale this would partition the pool into challenges provably out of reach at a given length and those merely unreached, a sharper object than a leaderboard position. It does not score: the fifth architecture here to concentrate its budget and change nothing.


6. TWO FURTHER NEGATIVE RESULTS

The abelianisation test is sound but empty here. A balanced presentation of the trivial group has exponent-sum matrix of determinant plus or minus one. Zero of the 7,266 solved challenges violate this, confirming the test, but all 2,756 unsolved already satisfy it. The pool was evidently pre-filtered.

The exponent-sum lower bound is admissible and far too weak. Across 24,080 matrices its median is 5 and its maximum 13, while held records have median above 100. It prunes nothing.


WHAT REMAINS OPEN

Every method measured is competent exactly where the optimum is short; the long-record presentations are those where no method reached any path at all. That region holds, by section 5, essentially all the point value. Five architectures were measured there, capped bidirectional, best-first with a depth weight, weighted A* on a learned heuristic, IDA*, and decision-mode IDA*, and all score zero, so more budget will not close it.

A learned search policy is the relevant class of method. As a first probe, a least-squares estimator of distance to the trivial pair, fitted on record-length paths, reached mean absolute error 3.0 moves against 5.6 for L(P) alone, and cut node counts roughly twentyfold as an A* heuristic, but did not improve path quality over the tuned priority of section 1. The useful policy is evidently not linear in simple word statistics.


PRIOR WORK USED, AND WHAT IS USED FROM IT

Official SAIR Andrews-Curtis repository, github.com/SAIRcompetition/Andrews-Curtis: move specification, challenge data and reference verifier, against which every path here was replayed. The tie rule of section 5 is the competition's own.

J. J. Andrews and M. L. Curtis, "Free groups and handlebodies", Proc. Amer. Math. Soc., 1965: the conjecture and the move set searched over.

C. F. Miller III and P. E. Schupp, "Some presentations of the trivial group", Contemp. Math., 1999: the Miller-Schupp family, part of the pool that sections 4 and 5 stratify.

A. Shehper, A. M. Medina-Mardones, B. Lewis, L. Pinar, S. Gukov and co-authors, "What makes math problems hard for reinforcement learning: a case study", arXiv:2408.15332: the yardstick for greedy baseline coverage on Miller-Schupp, and the reference for the learned-policy direction above. That greedy solves much of that family is what made its failure here worth reporting rather than assuming.

I. Pohl, "Bi-directional search", 1971, and "Heuristic search viewed as path finding in a graph", 1970; R. E. Korf, "Depth-first iterative-deepening", 1985: the bidirectional formulation of section 3, the weighted A* of section 5, and the memory-free search of section 5b. All used as given, none claimed as new.

CONTRIBUTION: the measurements, and five findings. The length cap rather than the budget defeats this search (section 1). The band structure any single priority setting imposes (section 2). The presentation-independence of the backward ball, and the bound on what precomputing it buys (section 3). The anti-correlation of competence and point value under the tie rule (section 5). The non-existence statement for ac-07351 (section 5b). The section 6 negatives and the closing probe are also original, reported because they bound the approach.
